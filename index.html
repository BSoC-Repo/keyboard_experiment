1Introduction

Conducting research, and analysing and presenting its findings, is a fundamental part of study in higher education. This session gives you first-hand experience of each of these stages within a single, focused experiment.

The experiment compares two mobile keyboard layouts, QWERTY and multi-tap, using low-fidelity cardboard prototypes. Working in pairs, one person enters a short phrase on each prototype while the other times the trial and records any errors. The data you collect will form the basis of an academic poster and a short video presentation.

The design is loosely based on MacKenzie and Read (2007), who showed that paper mockups can be used to evaluate keyboard layouts in a classroom setting, and that the results follow the same pattern as studies using working software.

Before the session: read the key concepts, try the simulator and find your group number. During the session: use the stopwatch and the results table. After the session: use the analysis guidance and the assessment checklists.

3Key concepts

Select each card to expand it.

Keystrokes per character (KSPC)

KSPC is the average number of key presses needed to produce one character of text. It is a simple way to compare the efficiency of different text entry methods.

KSPC = total key presses ÷ characters entered

QWERTY has one key per letter, so KSPC is about 1.0. Multi-tap places several letters on each key, and the key is pressed up to four times to reach the required letter, giving a KSPC of about 2.0. For example, "c" needs three presses of the 2 key, and "s" needs four presses of the 7 key.

A higher KSPC generally means slower entry and more opportunities for error, although other factors such as familiarity and key size also play a part.

Low-fidelity prototyping

A low-fidelity prototype is a quick, inexpensive representation of a design, such as a paper sketch or a cardboard mockup. It allows designers to evaluate ideas with users before investing time and money in building a working system.

Cardboard prototypes cannot respond to input, so a human observer plays the part of the system by watching and recording. This approach makes it easy to change a design and test again, but it removes realistic features such as tactile and visual feedback.

Fitts's Law

Fitts's Law predicts that the time taken to move to and select a target depends on the distance to the target and its size. Larger, closer targets are faster to select; smaller, more distant targets are slower.

MT = a + b × log2(D ÷ W + 1)

Here, MT is movement time, D is distance, W is target width, and a and b are constants found by experiment. A multi-tap keypad has fewer, larger keys than a QWERTY layout of the same size, which reduces movement time per press. This partly offsets the extra presses that multi-tap requires.

Keystroke-Level Model (KLM)

The Keystroke-Level Model predicts how long an expert will take to complete a task by adding together the times of its individual actions. Typical operators include K (pressing a key), P (pointing to a target), H (moving the hand between devices) and M (mental preparation).

For this experiment, a simple KLM estimate is the number of key presses multiplied by the average time per press. Because multi-tap needs roughly twice as many presses, KLM predicts that it will take roughly twice as long, if each press takes a similar time.

Learning effect

Participants usually improve across repeated trials as they become familiar with the task, the phrase and the layout. This is known as a learning effect.

Learning effects can distort comparisons between conditions. If every participant used multi-tap second, any improvement from practice would unfairly favour multi-tap. This experiment reduces the problem through a practice run, counterbalancing and matched pangrams. Comparing trial 1 with trial 3 also lets you measure the learning effect directly.

5Research question and hypothesis

Research question

How does a multi-tap layout compare with a QWERTY layout for text entry speed, accuracy and perceived workload?

Hypothesis

QWERTY will produce faster entry times and fewer errors, because it requires approximately half the key presses per character.

A good hypothesis is testable and states a direction. Notice that this one makes a prediction about two measurable outcomes (time and errors) and gives a reason grounded in theory (KSPC).

7Pangrams

A pangram is a sentence that contains every letter of the alphabet at least once. Using pangrams ensures that every key on each layout is used during a trial.

Pangram A

sphinx of black quartz judge my vow

Pangram B

how vexingly quick daft zebras jump

Each chip shows a character and the number of multi-tap presses it needs. Darker chips need more presses. A dashed outline marks a letter on the same key as the letter before it, so the participant must pause before entering it. Spaces are shown as ␣.

Why use two pangrams? If participants entered the same phrase on both devices, the second device would benefit from memorisation of the phrase. Using a different pangram for each device reduces this carry-over effect.

Why match them? Both pangrams have 35 characters, and they need almost the same number of multi-tap presses (68 and 69). Matching length and key presses means that any difference in performance can be attributed to the layout rather than to one phrase being harder than the other.

9Roles

Participant

Taps out the pangram on the cardboard prototype, saying each letter aloud. Speaking aloud allows the observer to follow the entry and spot errors, since the cardboard cannot display any text.

Observer

Times each trial, counts errors and records results in the survey. The observer starts timing on the first tap and stops on the final letter, and should remain consistent across every trial.

What counts as an error?

  • Wrong key
    The participant presses a key that does not contain the intended letter.
  • Missed letter
    A letter in the pangram is skipped.
  • Missed space
    The space key is not pressed between words.
  • Wrong number of presses
    On multi-tap, the key is pressed too few or too many times.

12The survey

The Microsoft Forms survey collects both quantitative data (times, errors and ratings) and qualitative data (comments and preferences). It has three parts.

Part A

Prior experience

Completed before the experiment. Records your experience with QWERTY and multi-tap keyboards, which helps to explain differences between participants.

Part B

Results and workload

Completed after each device. Records times and errors, along with workload ratings adapted from the NASA-TLX (Hart and Staveland, 1988), such as mental demand, physical demand, effort and frustration.

Part C

Preference and feedback

Completed after both devices. Records which layout you preferred, and why, along with suggestions for improving each design.

13Analysis and discussion

Comparing your results

  • Compare the mean WPM and total errors for each device. Which layout was faster, and by how much as a percentage?
  • Compare the size of the difference with the KSPC prediction. Multi-tap needs about 1.95 presses per character, so if each press took the same time, multi-tap WPM would be roughly half of QWERTY WPM.
  • Look at the change from trial 1 to trial 3. Is the learning effect larger for one layout?
  • Combine your data with the class data to obtain a larger sample before drawing conclusions.

Comparing with published research

MacKenzie and Read (2007) found that QWERTY was about 48% faster than a phone keypad, with mean entry rates of 28.1 WPM compared with 19.0 WPM.

Important difference: their phone keypad assumed predictive text with one press per letter, whereas this experiment uses multi-tap. You should therefore expect a larger gap between the two layouts in your results. Consider this carefully when comparing your findings with theirs.

Discussion prompts

  • Did the results support the hypothesis? Explain how the evidence supports or contradicts it.
  • How did the workload ratings differ between the layouts, and do they match the performance data?
  • What did participants say in their qualitative feedback, and does it help to explain the numbers?
  • Would the same pattern appear on a working phone? Justify your answer.

Limitations to consider

  • Prior familiarity: most participants use QWERTY daily, which favours that layout.
  • No tactile feedback: cardboard keys do not click or display letters, so errors may go unnoticed.
  • Observer judgement: errors are counted by eye and ear, and observers may disagree.
  • Small sample: a single pair, or even a single class, may not represent the wider population.
  • Hand timing: reaction time when starting and stopping the stopwatch adds error to every measurement.
  • Pangram matching: the pangrams are matched for length and presses, but Pangram A contains three pairs of consecutive letters on the same key (requiring a pause on multi-tap) while Pangram B contains none.

Suggested follow-up study: add a third condition using an alphabetical layout (one key per letter, A to Z). Like QWERTY, it has a KSPC of 1.0, but it is unfamiliar to participants. Comparing all three layouts would help to separate the effect of familiarity from the effect of key presses.
